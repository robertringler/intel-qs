# 1. Executive Finding

## The opportunity

**Become the origination, underwriting, and eventually the clearing layer for *located firm electrical capacity*: the enforceable right to consume a specified number of megawatts, at a specified place, beginning on a specified date, with a specified firmness — together with the load-flexibility commitments that manufacture that right out of existing grid headroom.**

Call it a **power-rights desk that becomes a power-rights market.**

## The economic function

Matching electrical capacity to electrical demand across time, place and firmness. This function is currently performed by ~3,000 US utilities, 7 RTOs/ISOs, 50 state commissions and thousands of counties, using engineering studies, spreadsheets, bilateral phone calls and five-year queues. It is the gating function on the largest private capital program in economic history, and it has no market, no standard product definition, no price transparency and no clearing mechanism.

## The initial wedge

A free, public, parcel-level answer to one question no one can currently answer reliably: **"Where in this ISO can 50–300 MW actually be energized within 24–36 months, and what would it take?"** Built entirely from public data at $0 cost. Published for free to buy distribution. Monetized first as paid site screening, then as a per-MW success fee on brokered transactions, then as a share of flexibility value, then as ownership of contracted capacity financed by third parties.

## Why the timing matters

Four clocks converged in 2025–2026 and none of them can be unwound quickly:

- **Demand inflected after 15 flat years.** US electricity consumption was approximately flat from 2005 to 2020 and is now rising `[FACT — EIA]`; EIA projects 4,135 BkWh in 2026 and 4,211 BkWh in 2027, with the commercial sector outgrowing residential **for the first time on record** `[FORECAST — EIA STEO Sept 2026]`.
- **The constraint moved from capital to capacity.** Big-5 hyperscaler capex is running at roughly $700–800B in 2026 `[ESTIMATE — J.P. Morgan $697B; trackers $775–800B]` against transformer lead times of 128–160+ weeks `[FACT]` and a 61-month median interconnection wait `[FACT — LBNL, VERIFY]`.
- **The right became separately priced.** Powered land in primary US markets traded at **$584,000 per MW in 2026, up 51% year over year and 35% above its five-year average** `[FACT — Cushman & Wakefield, 2026 Data Center Development Cost Guide]`. The market is now explicitly pricing the bare right to electricity, stripped of buildings, chips and tenants.
- **Regulators are writing the product definition right now.** FERC issued **Show Cause Orders to all six jurisdictional RTOs/ISOs on 18 June 2026** compelling them to justify or reform large-load interconnection tariffs `[FACT]`, having found PJM's co-located-load tariff unjust and unreasonable in December 2025 `[FACT]`. Order 2222 DER-aggregation implementations land between November 2026 and 2030 `[FACT]`.

## Why $0 is relevant — and not merely tolerable

This is the rare large opportunity where the binding input is *public-record synthesis plus relationship origination*, both of which cost nothing but labor. Every dataset required on day one is free: EIA Forms 860/861/923, FERC Form 715 and eLibrary, ISO queue and OASIS postings, utility hosting-capacity maps, state PUC dockets, county parcel records. More importantly, **the two assets that actually matter cannot be bought at any price** — a book of landowner relationships, and a private dataset of what utilities *actually did* versus what they published. Both must be accumulated transaction by transaction. Capital does not shortcut either. That is why a $0 entrant is not structurally disadvantaged here, which is unusual and is the core of the thesis.

## Potential scale

The underlying pool is unambiguously $1T+ annually: global electricity-sector investment is ~$2.2T in 2026 `[FACT — IEA]`, global electricity supply and infrastructure investment $1.6T (or $2.0T including end-use electrification) `[FACT — IEA]`, and global retail electricity spending is on the order of $3.1–3.7T/yr `[INFERENCE — arithmetic in §4]`.

Enterprise value is a different question, and here I will not flatter the thesis. **A $100–300B enterprise is the defensible base case. $1T is conditional, and the conditions are demanding** — it requires the business to complete a transition from asset-light origination to large-scale ownership of contracted capacity, financed by lenders and offtakers rather than by the founder. The full arithmetic, including what has to be true, is in §17.

## Primary uncertainty

**The single greatest risk is not competition or technology. It is that FERC's own 2026 reforms mandate the transparency that the first-stage business sells.** If all six RTOs are compelled to publish standardized, machine-readable hosting capacity, queue status and large-load interconnection terms, the information arbitrage compresses within 24–36 months. §18 treats this seriously, and §12 explains why I nonetheless believe it strengthens the transaction layer while destroying the data-subscription layer — and why you therefore must not build the data-subscription layer.

# 3. Why This Opportunity Exists NOW

Five inflection points, each independently sourced. None is a forecast of a new technology; all are observed changes in already-deployed systems.

## 3.1 Technological inflection: demand per unit of economic output broke trend

The relevant inflection is not that AI exists. It is that **AI converted a flat-demand industry into a growth industry within roughly 36 months**, and electricity infrastructure has 5-to-20-year build cycles. US electricity consumption changed little for more than a decade before 2020 `[FACT — EIA]`; it now reaches record levels, "driven by data center development and increased manufacturing activity" `[FORECAST — EIA STEO]`. Globally, IEA puts consumption at 28,600 TWh in 2025 rising to 30,700 TWh in 2027 `[FACT/FORECAST — IEA]`, with demand growth of 3.3% (2025) and 3.7% (2026) — "some of the highest growth rates observed over the last decade" `[FACT — IEA]`.

The second-order consequence is what matters: when a 20-year-cycle industry is asked to absorb a 3-year demand shock, **the scarce good stops being the commodity (kWh) and becomes the option (the right to a kW at a place and date).** Options markets require intermediaries. There is no intermediary.

## 3.2 Economic inflection: the capital is already committed and is waiting on permits

- Big-5 hyperscaler 2026 capex: ~$775–800B `[ESTIMATE — trackers]`; J.P. Morgan's figure is $697B `[ESTIMATE]`. Either way it exceeds the GDP of Switzerland.
- Morgan Stanley and J.P. Morgan estimate the sector may need to issue **~$1.5T of new debt** to finance it `[ESTIMATE]`.
- Yet **30–50% of hyperscaler data center sites are at risk of delay or cancellation** on transformer and switchgear backlogs `[ESTIMATE — industry]`, and only about one-third of the ~12 GW 2026 pipeline is actively under construction `[ESTIMATE]`.

This is the defining asymmetry of the moment: **capital is abundant and permission is scarce.** Historically, intermediaries who hold the scarce side of such an asymmetry capture extraordinary rents.

## 3.3 Regulatory inflection: the product is being legally defined this year

This is the most underappreciated fact in the dossier, and the strongest argument for timing.

| Action | Date | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| DOE ANOPR directing FERC to enable "timely, orderly, and non-discriminatory" large-load interconnection | 23 Oct 2025 `[FACT]` | Starts the federal process |
| FERC finds PJM tariff unjust/unreasonable as applied to generators serving co-located load | Dec 2025 `[FACT]` | Co-located load becomes a regulated product |
| FERC order partially accepting/rejecting PJM co-location compliance | 16 Apr 2026 `[FACT]` | Terms still unsettled |
| **FERC Show Cause Orders to all six jurisdictional ISOs/RTOs on large-load interconnection** | **18 Jun 2026** `[FACT]` | Every regional ruleset reopened simultaneously |
| Order 2222 implementations: ISO-NE energy/AS 1 Nov 2026; NYISO end-2026; PJM capacity in 2028/29 BRA (held Jun 2026), E&AS 1 Feb 2028; MISO 1 Jun 2029; SPP Q2 2030 | 2026–2030 `[FACT]` | Distributed flexibility becomes a sellable wholesale product, region by region |

FERC explicitly chose Show Cause Orders over traditional rulemaking because rulemaking "would be too inefficient" `[FACT]`. Translation: the agency is in a hurry, and the rules for the largest new load class in a century will be rewritten over roughly 12–24 months. **Regulatory change of this kind reliably creates intermediary demand**, because every counterparty needs someone who knows what the new rules permit before the incumbents' systems have been updated.

Note the counterintuitive gift in **Order 2023**: FERC eliminated the option to post a deposit in lieu of demonstrating site control (90% at request, 100% at facilities-study execution) and imposed a transitional withdrawal penalty of **nine times study cost** `[FACT]`. This deliberately excludes pure financial speculators from the queue. The effect is that **only parties combining land control with grid knowledge can originate**, which is precisely why this market has stayed illiquid — and precisely the position a $0 agent can occupy, because land control can be obtained for $0 via an exclusive marketing agreement with a landowner.

## 3.4 Physical inflection: the supply chain cannot respond for 3–5 years

- Substation transformer lead times: ~140 weeks (2023) → **160+ weeks (2026)** `[FACT]`; power transformers ~128 weeks; generator step-up units ~144 weeks `[FACT]`. USITC data cited as showing delivery timelines extending to **5 years**, from 24–30 months pre-2020 `[ESTIMATE — VERIFY]`.
- ERCOT's large-load interconnection queue went from **63 GW to 226 GW in a single year** `[FACT — as reported]`.
- Consequence: **self-supply.** 59 data centers totalling ~**90 GW** plan behind-the-meter generation — more than 25% of all planned US data center capacity `[ESTIMATE — Cleanview]`. But only ~**2 GW is actually online** as of mid-2026, ~3 GW expected by year-end `[ESTIMATE]`. 75% of identified behind-the-meter generation is gas `[ESTIMATE]`.

The 90 GW planned versus 2 GW delivered gap is the market failure in one number.

## 3.5 Geopolitical and fiscal inflection: this spending is administration-proof

Electricity capacity sits at the intersection of four separately-funded political priorities — AI competitiveness with China, manufacturing reshoring, grid reliability, and energy security. IEA puts 2026 grid investment at **$550B** globally `[FACT]`. DOE is pushing FERC to *accelerate* data center interconnection `[FACT]`, i.e. the federal executive is actively intervening on the demand side. There is no plausible US political configuration in which "more deliverable electricity" becomes unpopular, even as *who pays for it* becomes extremely contested (see §14 and §18).

# 5. $0 Entry Strategy

Concrete, and tested against the brief's filter: no laboratory, no fab, no facility, no inventory, no team, no proprietary data purchase, no pre-revenue regulatory approval, no advertising, no institutional capital to test demand.

### Day 1
Choose **one** market. Recommended: **ERCOT** (fastest interconnection, largest large-load queue at 226 GW, most transparent data, no capacity market to complicate the flexibility story) or **MISO/PJM-West** (highest capacity prices at the cap, therefore highest flexibility value). Do not start national; the entire edge is depth.

Download, at zero cost: the ISO interconnection queue and OASIS postings; EIA Forms 860, 861, 923; FERC Form 715 and relevant eLibrary dockets; the incumbent utilities' hosting-capacity maps; state PUC docket filings on large-load tariffs; county assessor parcel data; USGS/FCC fiber and terrain layers.

Build v0 of one artifact — **the Deliverability Score**: for each substation and each candidate parcel, an estimate of (a) MW of headroom, (b) earliest credible energization date, (c) the specific blocking item, (d) whether a flexibility commitment would remove the block, and (e) the named counterparty who decides. Open-source tooling only (Python, PostGIS, QGIS, DuckDB — all free).

### Day 7
**Publish it free.** A single public artifact: *"The 25 substations in [ISO] with more than 100 MW of apparent headroom and no queued project — and what it would take to energize each."*

This is the highest-leverage $0 action available, for four reasons: it costs nothing; it is self-authenticating (developers can check it against what they already know); it reaches exactly the few hundred people who matter; and **it inverts the sales motion** — instead of pitching strangers, the buyers who recognize its accuracy contact you. Distribution bought with analysis rather than money.

### Day 30
Fifty outbound conversations, in this priority order: mid-market and "neocloud" data center developers needing 50–300 MW (the hyperscalers have internal teams; the mid-market has nobody); EPCs and electrical contractors; industrial site-selection consultants; and — critically — **landowners** adjacent to the substations the Day 7 artifact identified, who do not know their land has become valuable.

Sell the first paid product: a **site screen** at $5,000–$25,000 per engagement. This is a service, delivered manually, with no technology. It is exactly the "embarrassingly manual version" the brief demands.

### Day 90
- **Three to ten exclusive marketing agreements with landowners.** Zero cash: you are their agent, compensated on success. This is the single most important $0 action in the entire plan, because Order 2023's site-control rule makes land control the prerequisite for any interconnection position, and an exclusive right-to-market is site control's cheapest precursor.
- First brokered transaction under LOI: match a developer to a parcel with a credible power path, for a success fee.
- Begin the proprietary dataset that matters: **realized outcomes** — for every inquiry made, what the utility actually quoted, actually scheduled, actually charged, and whether it held. Nothing in the public record contains this.

### Year 1
- Revenue: **$250k–$1.5M** `[HYPOTHESIS]`, from 10–30 paid screens plus one to three success fees on 20–100 MW at $2,000–$15,000/MW.
- Assets: the deliverability model, 20–50 landowner agreements, a realized-outcome dataset no competitor has, and a reputation in one ISO.
- Headcount: founder, plus one analyst paid from revenue.
- Outside capital raised: **zero required.**

# 8. $1M → $100M

Three stacked mechanisms, each unlocked by the prior one's asset.

### Mechanism 1: Origination at scale ($1M → $10M)

Replicate the ISO playbook across 4–6 regions. The model is portable; the relationships are not, so this requires 1–2 originators per region hired from revenue (typical profile: ex-utility interconnection engineer, ex-developer land agent).

Unit economics: $2,000–$15,000 per MW of originated capacity `[INFERENCE — a 1–3% success fee against the observed $584,000/MW powered-land price, consistent with real-estate and M&A success-fee norms of 1–5%]`. At 1,000 MW/yr originated and $6,000/MW blended, that is **$6M/yr**. Add retainers and landowner-side commissions: **$10M/yr**. Gross margin ~55–65% (people-heavy).

### Mechanism 2: Energy supply brokerage attached to every origination ($10M → $40M)

Every site you originate needs supply. Retail energy brokerage pays a residual of **1–5 mils/kWh ($0.001–0.005)**, embedded in the supplier's rate, paid monthly on metered consumption for the contract term `[FACT — industry standard]`.

`[INFERENCE]` A 1 GW load at 85% load factor consumes ~7.4 TWh/yr. At 2 mils: **$14.9M/yr recurring, from one customer.** At 5 mils: $37M/yr.

This is the quiet engine of the whole plan. It converts one-time origination fees into multi-year recurring revenue with near-100% incremental margin and no capital. At 3 GW of brokered load — a small fraction of the 226 GW in ERCOT's queue alone — this is **$45M/yr at 2 mils**.

### Mechanism 3: Flexibility aggregation ($40M → $100M+)

This is where origination becomes infrastructure, and it is enabled by regulation, not technology.

The Duke Nicholas Institute "Rethinking Load Growth" study finds that **76–100 GW of new large load** could be interconnected using existing grid capacity if loads accept curtailment averaging ~2 hours per event and ~0.25–0.5% of annual uptime `[ESTIMATE — academic, Duke Nicholas Institute]`. Simultaneously, DOE targets tripling VPP capacity from ~30 GW (2024) to **80–160 GW by 2030**, which it estimates could save ~$10B/yr in grid costs and meet 10–20% of peak demand `[ESTIMATE/FORECAST — DOE]`. Order 2222 makes aggregations ≥100 kW sellable into wholesale markets, region by region, 2026–2030 `[FACT]`.

So the product is: **you got them the site; now you monetize the flexibility commitment that made the site possible.** You hold the dispatch relationship.

Value: PJM capacity clears at **$118,625/MW-yr** `[FACT]`. On a 20–40% share of flexibility value, that is **$24,000–$48,000 per MW-yr** `[INFERENCE]` on managed capacity, with software-like margins once the dispatch platform exists.

`[INFERENCE]` 2,000 MW under flexibility management × $30,000/MW-yr = **$60M/yr at 70–80% gross margin.**

**Combined at year ~7–9:** ~$10M origination + ~$45M brokerage residual + ~$60M flexibility = **$115M/yr**, blended gross margin ~65%, with zero founder capital raised and only working capital financed — because origination fees are paid at closing, brokerage residuals require no capital, and flexibility revenue is settled by the ISO.

**The structural change across this phase:** you move from selling a view of the grid to *holding contractual rights over load*. The first is advice. The second is an asset that project lenders and ISOs must transact with.

# 9. $100M → $1T

I will not hand-wave this. **Three distinct structural transformations are required, and each one is a genuine discontinuity in the business — not a continuation.** If any fails, the business tops out somewhere between $5B and $50B of enterprise value, which would still be an excellent outcome and is the modal result.

### Transformation 1: From broker to balance sheet (the only path to $100B+)

An origination-and-flexibility platform earning $500M–$1B of revenue is worth perhaps $5–15B. To exceed $100B, the business must **own or co-own the contracted capacity it originates** — firmed, located, flexible supply under long-term contract.

This is the NextEra / Brookfield / Constellation / Equinix pattern, and critically **it is financed by other people**: project debt, infrastructure equity, and offtake prepayments. The evidence that this financing exists and is aggressive is direct: Crusoe, Blue Owl and Primary Digital Infrastructure announced a $3.4B / 206 MW joint venture, then a **$15B / 1.2 GW** venture with **J.P. Morgan leading a $7.1B construction loan** `[FACT — as reported]`. The pledged collateral in these deals "is neither the building nor the chips, but electricity in every form: a substation, a gas turbine, a position in an interconnection queue, a contract with a nuclear plant" `[FACT — as reported]`.

That sentence is the thesis restated by the credit market. **Located power rights are already collateral.** The originator who can underwrite them better than the lender can is positioned to own the equity.

`[INFERENCE]` 60 GW of contracted capacity at $250,000/MW-yr of EBITDA = $15B EBITDA; at a 12–15× infrastructure multiple = **$180–225B of enterprise value** (equity value materially lower after project leverage).

**Cost:** this transformation dilutes the founder severely and converts a 60%-margin asset-light business into a capital-intensive one with utility-like multiples. It is the right trade for scale and the wrong trade for returns-on-founder-equity. This tension is the central strategic decision of the entire plan, and §21 takes a position on it.

### Transformation 2: From participant to standard (the multiple expansion)

Enterprise value above ~$300B requires owning the *definition and settlement* of the product, not just a large inventory of it. Concretely: the standard contract for a located, firm, flexible capacity right, plus the registry that records it, plus the settlement engine that clears it between loads, generators, utilities and ISOs.

The regulatory window for this is open *now* and will not stay open: FERC is actively rewriting large-load interconnection terms across all six RTOs `[FACT]`, and Order 2222 compliance requires new registration, telemetry and settlement machinery in every region on a staggered 2026–2030 schedule `[FACT]`. Standards get set during rule transitions by whoever shows up with working implementations and data. After the transition, they ossify for decades.

`[HYPOTHESIS]` If the registry/settlement layer clears even 5% of global located-capacity transactions at a 10–25 bp fee, on the order of $1T+/yr of gross transaction value, that is $500M–$1.25B of extremely high-margin, extremely defensible revenue — worth $25–60B on its own but, more importantly, it is the asset that re-rates the *whole* enterprise from utility multiples to exchange multiples.

### Transformation 3: From national to global

The structure replicates wherever (a) electricity demand is growing faster than grid buildout, (b) a regulator permits third-party load aggregation, and (c) land and interconnection are separable rights. That is the US, Canada, the UK, EU, Japan, Korea, Australia, India, Brazil, the Gulf and increasingly Southeast Asia. Global electricity-sector investment of $2.2T/yr `[FACT — IEA]` is roughly 4× the US share, so the international multiple on any US-scale achievement is 3–4×.

**Honest assessment:** Transformation 1 has perhaps a 25–40% probability conditional on reaching $100M `[HYPOTHESIS]` — it mostly requires execution and a cooperative credit market. Transformation 2 is perhaps 10–20% `[HYPOTHESIS]` — standards positions are won rarely and partly by luck. Transformation 3 is 40–60% conditional on 1 `[HYPOTHESIS]`. **Compounded, the probability of the full $1T outcome is on the order of 2–5%.** That is not a reason to reject the plan — it is a reason to state it plainly, since the expected value is dominated by the 20–40% paths to $10–100B, which require no heroic assumptions at all.

# 11. Why Incumbents Haven't Already Won

**Mandatory section. "Nobody thought of it" is not available as an answer — and it isn't the answer. Many people have thought of it. Four structural reasons explain why none has captured it.**

### 11.1 The role conflict (the strongest reason)

The firms with the data cannot take the position, and the firms taking positions cannot get the data.

Enverus, LandGate and the other platforms sell to every side of the market. The moment such a firm takes a transaction fee or an equity position in a site, every other customer becomes a competitor and the subscription business dies. This is not a strategic error; it is a correct decision given their business model — and it permanently excludes them from the valuable layer. **LandGate's $12.2M of revenue after a decade with 30 billion data points is the empirical proof** `[ESTIMATE]`: the data-subscription ceiling in this market is tens of millions.

Conversely, developers and infrastructure funds *do* take positions, but vertically — they originate only for themselves. A developer has no incentive to build a horizontal origination platform that serves its competitors. So the horizontal transaction layer has no natural owner.

### 11.2 The asset did not exist until approximately 2023

You cannot build a market in a good that is not scarce. US electricity consumption changed little for more than a decade before 2020 `[FACT — EIA]`. In that world, interconnection was a *utility service you requested*, not a *right you competed for*. There was no price, because there was no shortage. The powered-land price series — $584,000/MW, **+51% in one year** `[FACT — Cushman & Wakefield]` — is roughly three years old as a meaningful signal. Markets form in the decade after the underlying good becomes scarce, not before. **This opportunity is approximately three years old, which is the answer to "why now."**

### 11.3 Jurisdictional fragmentation made the national view unsellable

~3,000 US utilities, 50 state commissions, 7 RTOs/ISOs, thousands of county permitting bodies. Assembling a national view is expensive and, until recently, no single customer needed one — a developer built in one or two states. The customer who needs 100 sites in 20 jurisdictions simultaneously appeared only when hyperscaler and neocloud buildouts went national, circa 2023–2024. **Demand for the product is newer than the product's feasibility.**

### 11.4 Regulation deliberately excluded the financial players

FERC Order 2023 eliminated deposits in lieu of site control (90% at request, 100% at facilities study) and imposed a 9× study-cost withdrawal penalty `[FACT]`, specifically to stop speculative queue entries. This worked: it kept hedge funds, land speculators and financial intermediaries out of the interconnection queue. The unintended consequence is that **the only parties who can originate are those who combine land control with grid engineering knowledge** — a combination that neither the data vendors (no land) nor the financiers (no engineering, barred from speculating) nor the utilities (cannot act commercially across territories) possess.

A $0 founder can assemble exactly that combination, because land control at the origination stage costs nothing: an exclusive marketing agreement with a landowner is a signature, not a cheque.

### 11.5 What would make me wrong about this section

If Enverus or a hyperscaler stands up a horizontal origination desk with transaction economics in the next 24 months, reason 11.1 dissolves. Watch Enverus's post-Pearl Street product direction specifically — a move from "data about sites" to "fees on sites" is the clearest possible falsification signal.

A better score wins more transactions. More transactions produce more observations. # 13. Network Effects

Four distinct effects, in order of arrival. I distinguish genuine network effects from mere scale economies, which are often conflated.

1. **Cross-side liquidity (true network effect, arrives ~$5–20M revenue).** Each additional landowner with verified headroom makes the platform more valuable to every developer, and vice versa. Classic two-sided marketplace dynamics. Weak early (thin both sides), strong once ~500 parcels and ~100 active buyers are present.

2. **Data network effect (true, arrives immediately, compounds forever).** Every transaction — including every *failed* one — improves the predictive model for all future users. Unlike most claimed "data moats," this one has a real mechanism: the labels (did the utility deliver on time, at the quoted cost?) are only observable to a party in the transaction.

3. **Flexibility portfolio effect (true, and economically the most powerful).** This is the one that justifies infrastructure valuation. A single flexible load can offer the grid an unreliable 50 MW. A portfolio of 50 geographically and temporally diversified loads can offer a *firm* 400 MW, because their curtailment constraints are uncorrelated. **Portfolio diversification converts low-value interruptible capacity into high-value firm capacity, and the conversion ratio improves with portfolio size.** Against PJM's $118,625/MW-yr capacity price `[FACT]`, this is a direct, quantifiable, superlinear return to scale. It is also the mechanism by which the Duke study's ~100 GW of curtailment-enabled headroom `[ESTIMATE]` becomes financeable.

4. **Standards effect (potential, late, uncertain).** If the contract template and registry become the market convention, every participant's adoption raises the cost of using anything else. High value, low probability.

**What is *not* a network effect here:** the public-data model. More users of a public-data product do not make it better. Anyone claiming otherwise is selling.

# 14. Regulatory Structure

## Risks

- **Mandated transparency is the existential risk to stage 1.** FERC's Show Cause Orders `[FACT]` could compel standardized publication of hosting capacity, queue status and large-load terms across all six RTOs. That would commoditize the information product within 24–36 months.
- **Cost-allocation backlash.** Behind-the-meter data center gas plants are already being argued to raise retail bills `[FACT — reported]`, and "who pays for data center infrastructure" is becoming a live state-level political fight. Outcome risk: large-load moratoria, punitive tariffs, or mandatory utility-controlled procurement that disintermediates brokers.
- **Order 2222 implementation drift.** MISO (1 Jun 2029) and SPP (Q2 2030) `[FACT]` are late; nearly five years after the order, implementation "remains slow" `[FACT]`. Flexibility revenue arrives on the regulator's schedule, not yours.
- **Licensing friction.** Retail energy brokerage requires state-by-state registration; ISO market participation requires creditworthiness and collateral; aggregation requires registration. Each is modest alone and a real drag collectively.
- **Antitrust exposure at scale.** A clearing position over located capacity would attract scrutiny — but only at a stage where that scrutiny signals success.
- **Securities-law exposure** if capacity rights are structured as tradable instruments. This is a real question for Transformation 2 and requires counsel, not optimism.

## Opportunities — regulation is net strongly positive here

- **Mandatory demand.** Order 2222 compliance creates registration, telemetry, metering and settlement obligations that someone must perform in every region `[FACT]`. Compliance obligations are the most durable form of demand there is.
- **A new product class, legally defined.** FERC's co-located-load determinations `[FACT]` and the Show Cause process are *creating* the firm-flexible-located-capacity product. Markets do not pre-exist their legal definitions.
- **Barriers to entry grow with time.** Registration in 7 ISOs and 50 states, built once, is a wall behind you.
- **A verification market.** Flexibility commitments must be measured, verified and settled. Measurement-and-verification regimes reliably create durable intermediary positions (the entire TIC industry, $254–408B of annual revenue by various private estimates `[ESTIMATE — unreliable, see preface]`, exists for this reason).
- **The transparency risk cuts the right way if you are positioned correctly.** See §18.1.

# 18. Red-Team Findings

I attempted to destroy the thesis. The five strongest arguments against it, with the evidence I found *for* each.

### 18.1 FERC mandates the transparency you sell — and the moat evaporates

**The case against:** The Show Cause Orders `[FACT]` are explicitly aimed at standardizing large-load interconnection terms across all six RTOs. DOE is pushing for acceleration `[FACT]`. The natural outcome is machine-readable publication of hosting capacity, queue status, and standardized large-load tariffs. Your Day 7 artifact becomes a government webpage.

**Severity: high for stage 1, and this is the most likely single cause of failure.**

**Why the thesis survives — partially.** Mandated transparency destroys the *data-subscription* business and leaves the *transaction* business stronger. The analogy is precise: public MLS data did not destroy real-estate brokerage; it destroyed the business of selling listings data and shifted all value to origination, relationships and execution. Transparency tells everyone *where* the headroom is; it does not tell them which landowner will sign, which flexibility structure the utility will actually accept, or whose dates hold. **The strategic implication is binding: never build the data-subscription company. Build the origination company, and treat the public data as a commodity input from day one.** A founder who misreads this and builds a data platform will produce a $12M business.

### 18.2 The AI capex cycle is a bubble and the demand signal is partly circular

**The case against, which is strong.** AI capex "has, in many cases, outpaced monetization" `[ESTIMATE]`. The sector may need ~$1.5T of new debt `[ESTIMATE]`. There is a structural lifecycle mismatch: GPUs last 3–7 years, buildings 20–30, creating a refinancing treadmill against depreciating collateral `[ESTIMATE]`. Interlocking commitments among NVIDIA, OpenAI, Oracle and CoreWeave — vendor equity, take-or-pay compute, debt-funded GPU purchases — "can make end-demand look larger and more independent than it is, echoing the Lucent/Nortel vendor-financing dynamics of 1999–2001" `[ESTIMATE — this is a serious analogy, not a throwaway]`.

**Severity: high for the growth rate; low for the thesis's existence.**

**Why the thesis survives.** Three reasons. (i) The observed scarcity is physical, not financial: 1.4% vacancy, 80.4% of construction preleased, sold-out HBM and CoWoS, 12–18-month GPU lead times `[FACT]`. (ii) **Electricity demand is diversified away from AI** — EIA attributes growth to data centers *and* manufacturing `[FORECAST]`, and electrification of transport, heat and industry continues regardless of AI. (iii) Most importantly, **the business is counter-cyclically hedged by construction**: in a bust, the scarce asset becomes *contracted, already-energized capacity*, which is exactly what the origination business accumulates. An AI capex collapse would crush the origination fee line and *increase* the value of held flexibility and contracted capacity. This is a genuine structural hedge, not a rationalization — but it would still cut the growth rate in half.

### 18.3 Emerald AI already won the flexibility layer

**The case against:** $150M Series A at a $1.05B valuation, >$220M total, NVIDIA and DCVC backing, a Digital Realty partnership on a ~100 MW power-flexible facility, and the same ">100 GW unlockable" thesis `[FACT]`.

**Severity: medium-high.**

**Why the thesis survives.** Emerald AI sells software *into* the data center to make an existing load flexible. It does not find the site, does not hold landowner relationships, does not broker the supply contract, and does not originate the interconnection. These are complements, not substitutes — a plausible outcome is that you become Emerald AI's origination channel, or vice versa. **But this is a real warning about sequencing:** if you attempt to lead with flexibility software, you lose to a company with $220M and NVIDIA's distribution. Lead with origination, where they are absent.

### 18.4 It is a people business with consulting multiples

**The case against:** Origination is relationship-driven, geographically fragmented, and hard to systematize. Energy brokerage is a commodity at 1–5 mils `[FACT]`. The gross margin degrades with headcount. Services companies trade at 1–3× revenue, not 10×. LandGate's decade-long $12.2M plateau is the cautionary case `[ESTIMATE]`.

**Severity: high — and this is the most likely outcome, as the downside case in §16 reflects.**

**Why the thesis partially survives.** The escape from consulting multiples runs through exactly two assets: the realized-outcome dataset (which makes origination systematically better rather than individually better) and the dispatch rights over flexible load (which are contractual and settle through the ISO, not through people). **If neither materializes by roughly $10M of revenue, the correct conclusion is that this is a $50–400M consulting business and should be run for cash, not scale.** That is a legitimate, non-catastrophic outcome, and recognizing it early is worth more than persisting.

### 18.5 Utilities and hyperscalers internalize the function

**The case against:** Utilities are the actual gatekeepers; hyperscalers have internal power teams and ~$700–800B of capex `[ESTIMATE]`; FERC is directing utilities to serve large loads faster. Why is there a role for an intermediary at all?

**Severity: medium.**

**Why the thesis survives.** Utilities are jurisdictionally bounded — none can originate across territories, which is precisely what a national developer needs. Hyperscalers internalize for themselves only, and are not the customer anyway. The addressable customer is the mid-market developer with hyperscaler-scale power problems and no power organization. **However**, this risk rises materially if hyperscalers begin selling power-origination services to the mid-market (plausible — they already resell compute), or if a large utility holding company builds a merchant origination arm.

### 18.6 Additional vulnerabilities found but not elaborated

- **Political cost-allocation backlash** could impose mandatory utility-controlled procurement, disintermediating brokers entirely `[HYPOTHESIS]`.
- **Order 2222 timelines could slip further** — five years in, implementation "remains slow" `[FACT]`, and MISO/SPP are at 2029–2030.
- **Key-person concentration**: the founder *is* the deliverability model for the first two years.
- **Credit requirements** for ISO market participation may be unreachable without capital, gating stage 3.

# 19. What Would Falsify the Thesis?

Concrete, measurable, time-bound. If these occur, abandon or restructure — do not rationalize.

**Fast falsifiers (0–6 months) — these test whether you should start at all:**

1. **60 days of outreach to landowners adjacent to identified high-headroom substations yields zero exclusive marketing agreements.** Kills the $0 origination wedge outright.
2. **The first 50 qualified conversations with 50–300 MW developers yield zero paid engagements at ≥$5,000.** Kills demonstrated willingness to pay.
3. **Three or more specific, non-obvious deliverability claims in your public artifact are refuted by practitioners.** Kills the premise that public data plus judgment produces a real edge.

**Structural falsifiers (1–4 years) — these test whether the market stays open:**

4. **Powered-land pricing declines year-over-year for two consecutive years** from the $584,000/MW 2026 level `[FACT baseline]`. The scarcity premium is the thesis.
5. **Median interconnection duration falls below 36 months by 2030** (from 61 months in 2025 `[FACT]`). Would indicate the coordination failure is being solved institutionally.
6. **Primary-market data center vacancy exceeds 8%** (from 1.4% `[FACT]`). Would indicate supply has caught demand.
7. **Hyperscaler capex falls more than 25% year-over-year and stays down for two years.** Halves the growth case (though see §18.2 on the hedge).
8. **All six RTOs publish standardized machine-readable hosting-capacity and large-load queue data AND no transaction-fee layer has emerged within 24 months of your start.** Means transparency arrived before you converted information into origination.
9. **Enverus, LandGate or a hyperscaler launches a horizontal origination desk taking per-MW transaction fees.** Dissolves the role-conflict opening of §11.1.
10. **Order 2222 flexibility revenue per MW in your markets comes in below $10,000/MW-yr** (versus the $24,000–48,000 modeled). Kills Mechanism 3 and caps the business at the downside case.

**The single most important metric to track:** powered-land price per MW, quarterly. It is the market's direct price for the asset this business originates. If it stops rising, everything downstream compresses.

# 20. Alternative Candidates

Five candidates that survived the full filter but did not become the primary thesis. **Not ranked.** Each is distinguished by what it does better than the winner, and by the specific constraint that disqualified it.

### 20.1 Verified resilience → MGA → insurance float

**What it does better than the winner:** the cleanest $0 start of any candidate, and the best financing architecture in existence. An MGA requires licences and carrier capacity but **no balance sheet** — commissions fund growth, and float eventually funds everything (the Berkshire and Progressive path). The US MGA market reached ~$128B of premium in 2025, with statutory DPW of $102.6B, up 12% `[ESTIMATE — Conning]`. The global protection gap is $1.83T in premium-equivalent terms `[ESTIMATE — Swiss Re]`, with the nat-cat gap alone growing 7% in 2025 to $424B `[ESTIMATE — Swiss Re]`. Global P&C is a $2.4T market `[ESTIMATE — Swiss Re]`.

**Why it did not win:** the moat is capital and regulatory licence, not data or network. Underwriting losses can destroy a decade of work in one season, and the founder controls neither the loss distribution nor the reinsurance cycle. The scaling mechanism requires *becoming a carrier*, which requires regulatory capital the founder cannot generate from commissions quickly enough. Strategically excellent; structurally fragile for a $0 entrant. **The strongest candidate if the founder's comparative advantage is underwriting judgement rather than systems.**

### 20.2 Verified human capability / outcome-priced labour

**What it does better:** the largest underlying pool on earth — global labour income dwarfs every other candidate, with the formal intermediation layer (staffing) at ~$650B globally in 2026 and ~$180B in the US `[ESTIMATE — SIA]`. And the $0 start is trivially real: a recruiting agency is the classic zero-capital, immediate-cash-flow business. The genuine inflection is that **AI makes work product evaluable at scale**, so competence may become measurable for the first time.

**Why it did not win:** no demonstrated willingness to pay for verification itself. Employers pay for *placement*, not for *measurement*; Microsoft/LinkedIn owns the credential graph; and competence measurement is contested, politically sensitive, and legally hazardous (disparate-impact exposure). Fails the "demonstrated willingness to pay" test at the layer that would be valuable. `[HYPOTHESIS — the inflection is real; the business model is not yet evidenced]`

### 20.3 Machine-to-machine payment, identity and authorisation rails

**What it does better:** the purest second-order AI play, with a theoretically unbounded transaction base — McKinsey projects $3–5T of agent-orchestrated retail spend by 2030 `[FORECAST — promotional, heavily discounted]`.

**Why it did not win decisively:** **the incumbents already took the standards.** Google's AP2 launched with 60+ partners including Mastercard, PayPal, Amex and Coinbase, and was contributed to the **FIDO Alliance in May 2026** `[FACT]`; OpenAI and Stripe have ACP; Coinbase has x402. Meanwhile actual volume is trivial: x402 had ~69,000 active agents, 165M transactions and ~**$50M cumulative** volume by late April 2026, averaging ~$0.30 per call `[FACT]`; Juniper estimates just $8B of global agentic spend in 2026 `[ESTIMATE]`. This fails criterion 7 (not owned by incumbents) and criterion 8 (new-entrant wedge) simultaneously. A $0 founder cannot win a standards war against Google, Stripe, Coinbase and Mastercard.

### 20.4 Post-quantum cryptographic migration and permanent cryptographic inventory

**What it does better:** the most *certain* demand of any candidate, because it is legally mandated with dates. New national-security-system acquisitions must be CNSA 2.0-compliant by **1 January 2027**, with full enforcement by **31 December 2031**; federal civilian key establishment by **31 December 2030** and signatures by **31 December 2031**; NSM-10 sets 2035 for all NSS; NIST IR 8547 proposes disallowing all quantum-vulnerable public-key algorithms by 2035 `[FACT]`. Mandatory demand with a statutory clock is the rarest thing in business.

**Why it did not win:** **it terminates.** The White House estimated government-wide migration at ~**$7.1B over 2025–2035** `[ESTIMATE]`. Even at 10–50× for the private sector, that is $70–350B *one-time*, not $1T annually. The durable residue — a permanent cryptographic asset inventory — is a real and attractive business, but it is a $1–10B company, not a $100B one. Fails criterion 1 on pool size and criterion 5 on structural persistence.

### 20.5 Healthcare administrative adjudication

**What it does better:** the largest single national waste pool identified anywhere in this research. US health spending reached **$5.3T in 2024, +7.2%** `[FACT — CMS]`, roughly 18% of GDP. Administrative costs run 8% of US care versus 1–3% in peer countries `[ESTIMATE]`, and the canonical waste estimate is **$760–935B annually, of which administrative complexity is ~$265.6B** `[ESTIMATE — Shrank et al.]`. The money is unambiguously there and unambiguously wasted.

**Why it did not win:** **incumbent resistance is the highest of any candidate examined.** The clearinghouse and adjudication layers are owned by UnitedHealth/Optum, Availity, Epic and Waystar, who are not merely entrenched but are *paid by the complexity itself*. The waste is not an accident awaiting a solution; it is several firms' revenue. Fails criterion 7 (not owned by incumbents) more clearly than anything else that reached the finals.

### 20.6 Rejected during screening, with reasons (abbreviated)

- **Trade/origin verification and tariff engineering** — the regulatory driver proved politically reversible (the Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs in February 2026, with $114.7B of refunds against $269.2B of gross FY2026 collections `[FACT]`), and the software layer is tiny at ~$2.19B in 2026 `[ESTIMATE]`.
- **AI training-data licensing clearinghouse** — the pool is too small. The largest disclosed deal is OpenAI/News Corp at ~$250M over five years; Wiley reported $49M of AI licensing revenue in FY2026; Google pays Reddit ~$60M/yr; the landmark Bartz v. Anthropic settlement was $1.5B, approved 20 July 2026 `[FACT]`. This is a $2–10B/yr pool, not $1T.
- **Testing, inspection and certification of AI outputs** — fragmented and low-margin; top seven firms hold only ~11% of the market `[ESTIMATE]`, and private sizings range $254–408B with no credible path to a dominant position.
- **Compute spot markets** — genuine structural openness, but non-fungibility (locality, interconnect, memory) prevents true commoditisation, and margins compress toward brokerage without the physical scarcity that makes the power thesis work.

# 21. Strategic Conclusion

> *"If I had $0 today and wanted to build toward the largest economically meaningful enterprise available to a new entrant over the next 20–30 years, what specific economic layer would I attempt to control first, and why?"*

**I would attempt to control the origination layer for located firm electrical capacity in one ISO, and I would build the realized-outcome dataset as the only strategic asset that matters.**

The investment-grade reasoning, in order of weight:

**1. The constraint has moved, and the market has not caught up.** For a century, capital was scarce and permission was abundant. Today roughly $700–800B of annual hyperscaler capex `[ESTIMATE]` queues behind a 61-month median interconnection wait `[FACT]`, a 13% historical completion rate `[FACT]`, and 128-to-160-week transformer lead times `[FACT]`. When the scarce factor changes, rents relocate to whoever controls the new scarce factor. The new scarce factor is the located right to consume electricity, and it is currently controlled by nobody — it is administered by 3,000 utilities and 50 commissions using engineering studies and phone calls.

**2. The right is already priced, already collateralized, and not yet traded.** Powered land at $584,000/MW, rising 51% in a year `[FACT]`. J.P. Morgan leading a $7.1B construction loan against a portfolio whose collateral is described as "electricity in every form: a substation, a gas turbine, a position in an interconnection queue" `[FACT]`. An asset that is priced and pledged but has no market, no standard contract and no clearing mechanism is an asset in the 3–7 years before its market forms. That is the window, and it is open now.

**3. The $0 constraint is not a handicap in this specific layer, which is the rarest feature of the analysis.** The required inputs are public-record synthesis and relationship origination. The two assets that compound — landowner agreements and realized-outcome data — **cannot be purchased at any price**, because one requires signatures and the other requires having been in the transactions. Capital accelerates almost every other opportunity in this dossier past a $0 founder. It does not accelerate this one. That asymmetry, not the market size, is the reason this candidate won.

**4. The structural openness has a named cause, not a gap in anyone's imagination.** Data vendors cannot take positions without destroying their business (LandGate: $12.2M of revenue after a decade with 30 billion data points `[ESTIMATE]`). Developers originate only for themselves. Financial speculators are barred by Order 2023's site-control rules `[FACT]`. Utilities cannot act across territories. The horizontal transaction layer has no natural owner — and that is a durable condition, not a temporary one.

**5. Regulation is writing the product definition during the window, and regulation is the most durable source of demand.** FERC's 18 June 2026 Show Cause Orders reopened large-load interconnection terms in all six jurisdictional RTOs simultaneously `[FACT]`; Order 2222 compliance creates registration, telemetry and settlement obligations on a staggered 2026–2030 schedule `[FACT]`. Standards are set during transitions by whoever arrives with working implementations, and then they hold for decades.

**6. And the discipline that makes or breaks it:** the value is in the transaction, never in the data. The single most probable failure mode — more likely than competition, bubbles, or regulatory change — is building the data-subscription company because it is the easier company to build. That path is already measured, and it terminates at roughly $12M of revenue.

**What I would not claim.** That this produces a trillion-dollar company. §17 shows the platform layer caps out near $430B even at an absurd 50% global share, and that ~75% of any trillion-dollar outcome would come from owning 150,000–200,000 MW of contracted capacity financed by third parties — which is achievable as an *enterprise* and dilutive to the point of near-irrelevance as *founder equity*. The defensible claim is this: **a $0 founder can credibly build toward a $10–50B enterprise in this layer with no outside capital required to prove demand, with a 20–40% probability conditional on competent execution, and with a 2–5% tail to the trillion-dollar outcome.** That is, so far as this research can determine, the best risk-adjusted answer available to someone starting with nothing.

**The constraint that prevents a cleaner "yes."** To be explicit, as the brief requires: no opportunity I examined fully satisfies "$0 → $1T" without qualification, and the binding constraint is not idea quality — it is **balance-sheet access**. Trillion-dollar enterprise value requires a trillion-dollar asset base or an exchange monopoly. A $0 founder can reach either only by financing the asset base with other people's capital, which dilutes them below the point where the trillion is personally meaningful, or by winning a standards position, which is low-probability and partly luck. **The opportunities that genuinely begin at $0 are those where the asset base is financed by counterparties — customers (prepayments), suppliers (project finance and offtake), policyholders (float), or users (network).** That observation is the most generalizable finding in this dossier, and it is why insurance float (§20.1) and project-financed capacity (the primary thesis) are the only two architectures that survived.

# Appendix A — The Candidate Universe and Successive Filters

# Appendix C — Macroeconomic Analysis: Where Capital Is Flowing, and Where It Is Being Misallocated

The brief asks a sharper question than most: not where capital is flowing, but **where it is being deployed inefficiently** — on the premise that the largest opportunity may sit where enormous sums are being spent badly. Answering it directly produced the thesis, so the reasoning belongs on the record.

### Where capital is flowing

| Flow | Scale | Label |
|---|---|---|
| Global energy investment | $3.4T/yr, +5% | `[FACT — IEA]` |
| → Electric power sector | ~$2.2T/yr (59%) | `[FACT — IEA]` |

### Where it is being misallocated — four measurable instances

**1. The interconnection queue is the largest documented capital-coordination failure in the economy.** Of all capacity that requested interconnection between 2000 and 2020, **only 13% reached commercial operation; 75% was withdrawn** `[FACT — LBNL]`. Each withdrawn request consumed development capital, engineering studies, legal work and land options. With 2,061 GW currently in active queues and 8,200 active projects `[FACT — LBNL]`, a 75% withdrawal rate implies that **the majority of all development capital deployed into US generation interconnection is destroyed before producing an electron.** This is not a market pricing risk correctly; it is a market with no price signal at all, because queue position cannot be valued, traded or underwritten.

**2. Capacity is being procured at administratively capped prices that are known to be too low.** PJM has cleared at the legislated cap for three consecutive auctions, and PJM's own simulation shows an uncapped market clearing **71% higher — $29.7B against the $16.4B buyers actually pay** `[ESTIMATE — PJM]`. A $13.3B/yr wedge between administered and market-clearing price is, by definition, suppressed investment signal: the shortfall shows up as capacity that does not get built.

**3. Roughly $700–800B/yr of capex is being committed against an input the committers cannot secure.** 30–50% of hyperscaler sites are at risk of delay or cancellation on equipment backlogs `[ESTIMATE]`; only about one-third of the ~12 GW 2026 pipeline is under construction `[ESTIMATE]`. Capital is being allocated on the assumption that power is procurable, when the evidence is that it is the binding constraint. The visible symptom is 59 projects totalling ~90 GW planning to build their own power plants — of which ~2 GW is actually operating `[ESTIMATE]`.

**4. The price signal that does exist is accelerating, which indicates the misallocation is widening rather than clearing.** Powered land at **$584,000/MW, +51% in a year** `[FACT — Cushman & Wakefield]`, against 1.4% vacancy and 80.4% of construction preleased `[FACT — VERIFY]`. A 51% annual appreciation in the price of a production input, sustained alongside record-low vacancy, is the signature of a market where supply cannot respond to price — i.e. where the constraint is coordination and permission, not capital.

### The inference that produced the thesis

`[INFERENCE]` When capital is abundant, permission is scarce, the scarce right is priced but not traded, and 75% of the capital deployed to obtain that right is destroyed in the attempt, **the highest-return available position is not to supply more capital — it is to reduce the failure rate.** The business that cuts a 75% withdrawal rate materially creates value measured in the hundreds of billions and can capture a fraction of it without owning any physical asset. That is a brokerage, and brokerages can be started with nothing.

### Macroeconomic conditions that support this specifically

- **Interest rates and capital cost:** higher-for-longer rates penalise long-duration, high-failure-rate development most severely, which raises the value of reducing time-to-energisation. A business paid for speed gains pricing power as rates rise — an unusual and valuable property.
- **Labour shortages:** the specialised skill (utility interconnection engineering) is in acute shortage, which protects origination margins and slows competitive replication.
- **Government debt and fiscal constraint:** governments cannot fund the grid buildout directly at $550B/yr globally, which forces reliance on private origination and third-party financing — the structure this business occupies.
- **Reshoring and defence spending:** both add industrial electrical load that is politically protected across administrations, diversifying the demand base away from AI (the hedge in §18.2).
- **Demographics:** ageing workforces in utilities mean institutional knowledge of interconnection is *leaving* the utilities over the next decade — which is, bluntly, the recruiting pipeline for this business and a further reason the incumbent capability is eroding rather than consolidating.
